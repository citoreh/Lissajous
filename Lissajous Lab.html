Presets (a : b, δ)

The ratio sets the shape

When a/b is a ratio of whole numbers the pen returns to its start and the curve closes. Small ratios like 1:2 or 3:2 give simple, open loops; larger ones such as 7:6 weave a dense mesh.

Count the touches

For a closed curve, count where it touches the right edge and where it touches the top edge. Right-edge touches equal a, top-edge touches equal b. Engineers used exactly this trick on oscilloscopes to compare an unknown frequency with a reference.

Phase turns it in depth

Sweeping δ makes the figure look like a 3-D wire rotating. At certain phases the forward and return paths overlap and the curve collapses into an open arc: 1:1 at δ = 0 is a straight line, and 1:2 at δ = π/4 is a parabola.

1 : 1 is the ellipse family

With equal frequencies you get an ellipse. At δ = π/2 and equal amplitudes it is a circle; at δ = 0 or π it flattens to a diagonal line. The tilt of the ellipse tells you the phase difference between two signals of the same frequency.

Irrational ratios never close

Tick “Non-integer frequencies” and set something like 1 : 1.41. The pen never repeats; given enough time it fills the whole rectangle. A near-whole ratio such as 2 : 1.01 looks like a slowly rotating 2:1 figure instead.

A little history

Nathaniel Bowditch studied these curves in 1815 using a compound pendulum. Jules Antoine Lissajous explored them in 1857 by bouncing light off mirrors fixed to two vibrating tuning forks, letting audiences see musical intervals: a perfect fifth (3:2) or an octave (2:1).
